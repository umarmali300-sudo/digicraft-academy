Sending many proposals and hearing nothing back is common, and it is not always a skills problem. A lot of proposals are long, generic, and about the freelancer instead of the job. Clients on Upwork, Fiverr, and in email skim the opening lines. This structure makes those lines useful. It does not guarantee a reply or a hire.

## Why proposals get skipped

- They start with the freelancer's name and years of experience before the client's problem.
- They are pasted unchanged into every job.
- They are so long that the actual offer is hard to find.

Fixing those three habits changes the odds. It does not control the client's decision.

## Four parts of a clearer proposal

### Open with their problem

The first sentence should show that you read the post.

Weaker: "Hello, my name is Ahmed and I am a professional graphic designer."

Clearer: "You need a Shopify product page that loads quickly for mobile visitors. I redesigned three similar pages last month and can start on yours this week."

Only use a claim like that if it is true.

### Give one proof, not a biography

Add one relevant project or one sample. A real result is better than "excellent results for previous clients." If you are new, a short sample made for this kind of job is honest proof. One relevant link beats a long client list.

### Add a two or three step plan

Show how the first days would work, with a timeline you can keep. For a writing job: study the brand and two competitors, send a draft within 48 hours, and revise within a day of feedback. "As soon as possible" is not a plan.

### Close with a small question

"I look forward to hearing from you" is easy to ignore. A specific question is easier to answer: "Do you want a casual or formal tone?" or "Should I send a 200-word sample on this topic?"

## Keep platform proposals short

On Upwork or Fiverr, about 150 to 300 words is enough if every sentence helps the client decide. Use short paragraphs. A cold email can be a little longer, in the same order: problem, proof, plan, question.

You can keep a template with three blanks: their problem in your words, the most relevant proof, and one question about their business. Also follow any instruction hidden in the post, such as a word they asked you to include. Missing it shows the post was not read.

## If you have no portfolio yet

Create a few labeled samples for businesses in the niche you want. A writer can rewrite a public landing section. A designer can show a concept. A developer can build a small demo. Say that they are samples. A small paid job with a real testimonial is stronger than a pile of concepts, and the testimonial has to be genuine.

## Price and follow-up

Include a price or a range so the client knows what they are considering. Tie it to the work, not an apology. If you follow up, wait several days and add something useful: a tighter scope, a relevant idea, or an answer to a detail in the post. After a few unanswered notes, stop.

## Before you send it

- The opening names their problem or the result they asked for
- One relevant proof or sample is included, and it is true
- A short plan has a timeline you can meet
- The ending asks one specific question
- A price or range is stated
- The proposal is short enough to skim
- At least one detail proves you read the post

A useful proposal makes the next step easy to understand. Whether the client replies depends on their budget, timing, and the other applicants. Nothing in this guide guarantees a client or an income.
